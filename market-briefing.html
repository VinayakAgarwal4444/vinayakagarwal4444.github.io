Market Briefing

A chat assistant for the last 48 hours of financial news. Ask what moved markets and why, and every claim in the answer links to the article it came from. Prices come from a live market feed, never from the model, and when the news doesn't cover a question it says so instead of guessing.

01 · At a glance

The short version

The problem

A language model can't know what happened yesterday. Asked about this week's markets, it either refuses or answers confidently from stale training data, and in finance a confident wrong number is the worst possible answer.

What I built

A pipeline that reads eleven news feeds every 20 minutes, groups articles into stories and finds the ones that answer a question. The model only has to read and cite; prices, advice requests and citation checks are handled in code.

What I found

With the news, the model got every expected fact in 28 of 28 answers; without it, 8. Measuring also killed my most obvious idea: ranking fresher stories higher made search worse, so the app doesn't do it.

02 · Architecture

The model reads; the code decides

Everything the model is trusted with is reading and summarising. The stories it may use are numbered [1], [2]…; prices it may quote arrive in a separate block cited [M]. After it answers, code checks that every citation points at something it was given and that every number appears in a source, and the app warns under any answer that fails.

Advice is handled the same way. “Should I buy…?” is caught by a pattern before the model sees it, the app shows a fixed notice, and the model is asked the neutral version: what has been reported?

03 · Methodology

How it was built

Five steps. Pick one to see the detail.

Read the news, keep only what's needed

Eleven free RSS feeds are read in parallel: three from CNBC, plus MarketWatch, the FT, the BBC, the Guardian, Nasdaq, Seeking Alpha, the Federal Reserve and the ECB. A news API was out: NewsAPI's free plan is a day behind, which defeats the point.

  • Headline, summary and link only. Articles are never stored, so every answer sends readers back to the publisher.
  • Cleaned on the way in: tracking parameters, inline CSS and boilerplate like “Continue reading” are stripped, and a summary that just repeats the headline is dropped.
  • Embedded once. Each item's vector is stored in SQLite next to it, so a refresh only embeds what's new. Anything older than 72 hours is pruned.

Group articles into stories

The same event is reported by five outlets within an hour. Without grouping, one story fills every search result. Articles whose headlines are at least 0.8 similar become one story, which the app ranks and cites as a unit.

  • Headlines, not summaries. My first version compared summaries, and a Guardian live blog that rounds up the whole day merged with every story it mentioned, including an unrelated crypto hack.
  • A ticker guard. Two earnings headlines differing only in the company, such as (CVCO) and (CVLG), scored as near-identical. Titles naming different tickers are never merged.

Find the stories that answer the question

Keyword search (BM25) catches exact names like “Okta” or “Series I bond”; embeddings catch paraphrases, so “chip stocks” finds “semiconductor shares”. The two rankings are merged with reciprocal-rank fusion.

  • A similarity floor stops loosely related stories reaching the model. That floor is what makes “nothing was reported” possible.
  • Abbreviations are expanded (“ECB” also searches “European Central Bank”). Without it, search missed the ECB's own rate decision.
  • Tickers and names are spotted (“Intel”, “$AMD”, “the S&P 500”) and their live quotes fetched for the answer.
  • Exact search, no vector database. A few hundred headlines fit in one matrix, so FAISS would add a dependency and buy nothing.

Answer, then check the answer

The prompt holds the numbered stories, the quotes and eight rules: cite every sentence, use no number that isn't in the sources, never advise. A small model doesn't follow all eight every time, so the important ones are also enforced after it answers.

  • No stories, no model call. If search finds nothing, the app says so without asking the model, which can't then invent something.
  • Citations checked. Any tag pointing at nothing it was given is flagged under the answer.
  • Numbers checked. Every figure in the answer must appear in its sources; the app warns about any that doesn't.
  • Follow-ups. “What about AMD?” is searched together with the previous question, but a complete question about another company isn't.

Test it on news that can't change

Live news changes every few minutes, so I froze one day of it (137 stories, 159 articles) and wrote 40 questions against it. The search settings and both models were measured on that snapshot.

  • 28 answerable, each with the story that answers it and the facts the answer must contain.
  • 8 uncovered: plausible questions the day's news doesn't answer, like the Bank of Japan or an Nvidia stock split. The right answer is “nothing reported”.
  • 4 advice requests, like “Should I buy Intel stock now?”
  • A closed-book baseline: the same questions to the same model with no news, to show what retrieval adds.

05 · Evaluation

What the numbers say

8/28 without the news

Retrieval is the product

Closed book, both models got every fact in only 8 of 28 answers. Llama admitted it lacked current information once; gpt-oss never did. The rest were confident answers from stale or invented facts.

27 → 21 ranked first

Fresher wasn't better

Boosting recent stories seemed obviously right for news. Measured, it pushed correct day-old stories down. Even a 5% weight cost two first places, so search doesn't use one; the 48-hour window keeps it recent.

6.6 → 3.0 stories of noise

Keywords let noise through

On uncovered questions, hybrid search first passed twice as many stories to the model as embeddings alone. Making keyword matches respect the similarity floor halved the noise with no loss of recall.

8/8 both models

It says when it doesn't know

Every uncovered question got “nothing in the last 48 hours of coverage addresses that”, and no answerable question was wrongly refused.

4/4 both models

No advice

All four advice requests were caught in code and answered with what was reported. I read each answer, because one passed the automated check while still saying “it's a buy” (see below).

0/40 both models

No invented sources or figures

No answer cited a story it wasn't given or stated a number found in none of its sources. That is a result for this run, not a guarantee, which is why both checks also run live.

06 · Challenges

What reading the answers found

The scores were high from early on. Reading the answers themselves turned up problems the scores missed, and each one is now handled in code.

  1. An invented story. After correctly saying “nothing was reported”, the model was allowed to point to the closest related story, and made one up: a Netflix quote cited as [1] when it had been given no stories at all. Now the model isn't called when search finds nothing, and only cited sentences survive after a “nothing reported”.
  2. An invented figure. A GDP answer added “better than the expected 2.8%”, a number in none of its sources. Every answer is now checked for numbers its sources don't contain.
  3. A retired model. The day I deployed, Groq had already retired Llama 3.1 8B and the app crashed on its first answer. I moved to gpt-oss-20b, made provider errors show a message instead of a crash, and ran the full evaluation again rather than assume the scores carried over.
  4. A new model, new habits. gpt-oss sometimes cites with 【1】 instead of [1], which left citations unlinked and unchecked; they're now converted first. It also summed analysts' views up as “the consensus is that Amazon is still a buy”: attributed, so the keyword check passed it, but still a recommendation. Advice questions now forbid that kind of verdict.
  5. A bug in my own test. A correct answer failed because gpt-oss wrote “North‑Korean” with a non-breaking hyphen. The fact check now ignores typography, and the write-up says so.

07 · Limits

What the checks still miss

  1. A real number, wrongly used. The briefing at the top of this page says Intel's fall wiped “roughly $12bn off the company's market value”. The source said $12 billion off the government's stake; the company lost about $140 billion. The number check passed it, because $12 billion is in a source. It can confirm a figure exists, not that it's attached to the right thing.
  2. Answers without citations. gpt-oss occasionally writes a correct answer with no citation tags at all. The app now says so under the answer instead of letting it look sourced, but the reader has to open the sources themselves.
  3. A small test. 40 questions on one day's news, and the search thresholds were tuned on that same set. The honest next step is a second snapshot from another day, scored without re-tuning.
  4. Headlines and summaries only. The model sees a sentence or two per article, enough for “what happened” but not for detailed analysis.

08 · Reflection

What I learned

Measure the obvious idea

The recency boost was the first thing I'd have added to any news search, and it made ranking worse. Without a fixed test set I'd have shipped it and never known.

Read the answers, not just the scores

An invented story, an invented figure and advice dressed up as a summary all passed the metrics I had at the time. Each one became a new check only because I read every answer.

Put the rules in code

A small model told “never give advice” and “cite every sentence” mostly complies. Mostly isn't enough for finance, so what must always hold is enforced after it answers, and survives a change of model.
