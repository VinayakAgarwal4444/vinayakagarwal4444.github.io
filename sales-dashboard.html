Retail Sales Dashboard

Fifteen months of sales from four stores and five products, turned into a six-page Power BI report that answers five business questions: how much we sold, what sold, where, to whom, and when it went well or badly.

01 · At a glance

The short version

The question

Where does the revenue actually come from, and is the business growing? The brief asked for totals by region, units by product, transactions by store and demographic, and the best and worst performers over time.

What I did

Modelled the data as a star schema with a full calendar table, and wrote 24 measures. The best/worst ones recalculate whenever you change a slicer. Then I laid the answers out across five themed pages plus a written summary page.

What I found

Electronics earns 81% of revenue on 60% of units. Price, not demand, decides the product ranking. And the headline “2023 is down 75%” is a trap: like for like, Q1 2023 is down 8.1%.

02 · The brief

Five business questions

Every page in the report exists to answer one of these. The last page answers all five in plain sentences, with every number traceable back to a measure.

  1. Q1 What are total sales, and how do they split by region?
  2. Q2 What is the total quantity sold, by category and by product?
  3. Q3 How many transactions were there, by store and by customer demographic?
  4. Q4 What performed best, and when?
  5. Q5 What performed worst, and when?

03 · Data model

A small, clean star schema

One fact table, Sales, holds a row per transaction: who bought, what, where, when, how many and at what price. Four dimension tables describe each of those keys. All four relationships are single-direction, many-to-one, so a slicer on any dimension filters the fact table and nothing filters back the wrong way.

04 · Methodology

How it was built

Five steps. Pick one to see the detail.

Prepare and model the data

The source workbook had customers, products, stores and sales on separate sheets. I loaded each into Power Query, set the data types (integer keys, decimal prices, true dates) and kept the tables narrow so each one does one job.

  • Used the Age Band column to group customers (25–34, 35–44, 45–54) rather than plotting every age.
  • Kept Price on every sale line instead of looking it up from Products, because the price paid can differ from the list price.
  • Joined the four dimensions to Sales on their IDs, many-to-one, single direction.

Build a proper date table in DAX

Time intelligence functions like TOTALYTD and DATEADD need a continuous date table with no gaps. The Date table follows this CALENDAR + GENERATE pattern and covers both full calendar years, one row per day, 730 in all. 

Date =
VAR DateList = CALENDAR ( DATE ( 2022, 1, 1 ), DATE ( 2023, 12, 31 ) )
RETURN
GENERATE (
    DateList,
    VAR d = [Date]
    RETURN ROW (
        "Month Number", MONTH ( d ),
        "Month Name", FORMAT ( d, "MMM" ),
        "Calendar Year", "CY " & YEAR ( d ),
        "Calendar Year Month Number", YEAR ( d ) * 100 + MONTH ( d ),
        "Day of Week Number", WEEKDAY ( d, 2 ),   -- Monday = 1
        "Quarter Name", FORMAT ( d, "\QQ" ),
        "Week Number", WEEKNUM ( d )
    )
)

The numeric helper columns (like Calendar Year Month Number) are there to sort the text labels, so “Feb 2022” comes after “Jan 2022” and not after “Dec 2022”.

Write the measures

All the logic lives in 24 explicit measures on the Sales table. No visual relies on Power BI's automatic summing. They fall into four groups:

Core

Total Sales · Total Qty · Number of Transactions · Avg Sales · Avg Qty per Transaction · Unique Customers

Time intelligence

YTD Sales · Comparable Sales · Last Year Sales · Vs Last Year Sales · Vs Last Year % · Weekend Sales %

Share and rank

Sales % of Total · Sales Rank

Dynamic best / worst

Best and Worst Product, Category, Store, Region and Month

Total Sales = SUMX ( Sales, Sales[Quantity] * Sales[Price] )

Last Year Sales =
VAR FirstSale = CALCULATE ( MIN ( Sales[Sale Date] ), REMOVEFILTERS () )
VAR LastMonthEnd = EOMONTH ( CALCULATE ( MAX ( Sales[Sale Date] ), REMOVEFILTERS () ), 0 )
-- only days that have data AND a full year of history behind them
VAR Days = FILTER ( VALUES ( 'Date'[Date] ),
    'Date'[Date] <= LastMonthEnd && EDATE ( 'Date'[Date], -12 ) >= FirstSale )
RETURN CALCULATE ( [Total Sales], REMOVEFILTERS ( 'Date' ), DATEADD ( Days, -1, YEAR ) )

Best Product =
VAR T = ADDCOLUMNS ( ALLSELECTED ( Products[Product Name] ), "@S", [Total Sales] )
VAR R = TOPN ( 1, FILTER ( T, NOT ISBLANK ( [@S] ) ), [@S], DESC )
RETURN CONCATENATEX ( R, Products[Product Name] & " (" & FORMAT ( [@S], "$#,0" ) & ")", ", " )

The best/worst pattern is the one I'm happiest with. ALLSELECTED keeps whatever the slicers have selected, so filtering to the West region shows the West's best product, not the overall winner. Swapping the column and the sort order gives the other nine measures.

Design the report

Six pages at 1280 × 720, all with the same layout: a title bar with a one-line takeaway, a row of slicers and KPI cards, then charts that go from general to specific.

  • Executive Overview: headline KPIs and a chart of each dimension.
  • Products & Categories: revenue against units, side by side, to show the price effect.
  • Stores & Geography: store map by latitude/longitude, a store × category matrix.
  • Customer Demographics: age band, gender, occupation and city.
  • Time Intelligence: YTD, vs last year, best/worst month, day of week.
  • Answers: the five questions answered in text, plus recommendations.

A ten-colour custom theme keeps each category the same colour across every page, and each page's subtitle tells you what to notice before you start reading the charts.

Validate before quoting anything

Before writing the Answers page I checked every figure against the raw rows, and that's where the most important finding came from.

  • The data ends on 30 Mar 2023. Full-year 2022 ($11.58M) against 2023 to date ($2.87M) looks like a 75% collapse. The fair comparison is Q1 against Q1: −8.1%.
  • The first version of the “Vs Last Year” card made the same mistake. With no year selected it compared all 15 months against 2022 and showed +24.8%. I rewrote Last Year Sales to use only days that have a year of history behind them, and added a matching Comparable Sales measure. The card now reads −8.1% with no year selected, and stays blank for 2022, which has no prior year to compare against.
  • The date table runs to Dec 2023, so the blank months after March are future dates, not missing data.
  • Each customer has a unique occupation and city in this dataset, so those two charts mirror each other. I noted that on the page so nobody reads it as two separate findings.

06 · Key findings

What the data says

81% on 60% of units

Electronics carries the business

Electronics brings in $11.67M of the $14.45M on 4,834 of 8,013 units. Clothing moves nearly as many units at about a third of the price, so it brings people in more than it brings in money.

14% spread in units

Price decides the ranking, not demand

All five products sold between 1,486 and 1,693 units. MacBook Pro sold the fewest electronics units and still made $5.42M (37.5%), the most of any product, purely on price.

11% store-to-store gap

No store is an outlier

Costco (594 transactions), Best Buy (593), Walmart (570) and Target (530) are close together. Changing store operations would move the total far less than changing the product mix or reaching new regions.

1 city

Midwest's gap is coverage, not demand

Midwest is only 10% of revenue, but it is a single city (Chicago) and has the second-highest average sale at $6,504. Adding cities there is the clearest growth lever in the data.

31.7% on 2 of 7 days

Weekends punch above their weight

Saturday and Sunday bring in nearly a third of revenue. Sunday alone is the best day ($2.39M); Thursday is the weakest ($1.81M). Staff and stock should follow that pattern.

−8.1% Q1 vs Q1

The real trend is a soft decline

January 2023 was the best month on record ($1.24M), but February and March were the second- and third-weakest. Q1 2023 ($2.87M) is down on Q1 2022 ($3.12M), with fewer transactions and a lower average sale.

07 · Recommendations

What I'd do with this

  1. Protect the top sellers. Keep MacBook Pro and iPhone X in stock before chasing Clothing volume. That's where the money is.
  2. Measure Clothing differently. Track it on transactions and add-on sales, not revenue.
  3. Look past individual stores. With an 11% spread, product mix and regional coverage matter far more.
  4. Expand in the Midwest. One city with high-value customers suggests demand that isn't being reached.
  5. Plan for weekends. Staff and stock to the Saturday/Sunday peak.
  6. Give Q1 2023 one more month. Watch April before calling the decline a trend.

08 · Reflection

What I learned

Check the date range first

The most misleading number in the report was one I almost published. Now I check the date coverage before comparing any two periods.

Measures over columns

Writing explicit measures, even simple ones like Total Qty, made every visual consistent and every number on the Answers page easy to trace back.

Context is the feature

ALLSELECTED made the best/worst cards respond to the slicers. The same function gives the right total for “% of total”, so the percentages stay correct when you filter.
