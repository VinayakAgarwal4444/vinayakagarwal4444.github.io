Data Jobs Analysis

A year of data job postings, 478,895 of them from 160 countries, turned into an interactive Power BI report. It answers the questions I had as someone looking for work in data: which roles pay best, which skills are in demand, and how much of it is remote.

01 · At a glance

The short version

The question

What does the market for data jobs actually look like? Which roles pay most, which skills keep showing up, and how often can you work from home or skip the degree requirement?

What I did

Loaded the postings with Power Query, then built one practice page for each family of Power BI visual. The last two pages are the real product: a dashboard you slice by role and a drill-through page with the detail for any one role.

What I found

Senior and ML roles pay about $155K, Data Analysts $90K. SQL and Python are each in half of all postings. And only 4% of postings list a yearly salary, mostly in the US, so the pay figures need a caveat.

02 · The brief

Questions a job seeker asks

I built this while job hunting, so the questions are the ones I wanted answered. The dashboard answers the first three for every role at once; the drill-through page answers the rest for whichever role you pick.

  1. Q1 How did the number of postings change over 2024?
  2. Q2 Which data roles are most in demand, and which pay best?
  3. Q3 How do hourly and yearly pay compare across roles?
  4. Q4 For one role: how often is it remote, degree-free, or with health insurance?
  5. Q5 For one role: where in the world are the jobs, and on which job boards?

03 · Data model

One wide table, kept honest

The source is a single CSV with one row per posting, already flattened: role, company, location, schedule, salary, and the list of skills the posting mentions. With one fact and no lookups to share, a star schema would have added joins without adding meaning, so I kept it as one table and let Power BI's date hierarchy handle the time axis. Two DAX calculated columns fix problems in the source without editing it (see step 05).

04 · Methodology

How it was built

Five steps. Pick one to see the detail.

Load and type the data

Nearly half a million rows is enough that types matter. In Power Query I set the posted date to a true date-time, both salary columns to decimals and the three yes/no columns to true/false, so that Power BI stores them compactly and charts them correctly.

  • Kept salary_year_avg and salary_hour_avg separate. Converting hourly to yearly means guessing the hours, and the two groups are different kinds of jobs.
  • Used job_title_short (10 clean roles) for every chart, and left the free-text job_title for tooltips.
  • Kept two tiny hand-made tables for the funnel and waterfall examples, since those charts need staged data the postings don't have.

Learn every visual on real data

Before designing the dashboard I built a practice page for each family of Power BI visual, all on the same postings. That way the dashboard uses each chart because it suits the question, not because it was the one I knew.

A Home page with a page navigator ties the twelve pages together, so the report can be explored without the Power BI page tabs.

Design the dashboard

One page, sliced by role. The top row answers “what does this pay?”, the charts underneath answer “how much demand is there, and when?”

  • Median, not average, for every salary card. A few very high salaries pull the average up.
  • A star rating measure turns the median salary into zero to five stars on a $75K–$150K scale, so roles can be compared at a glance.
  • The trend line uses the full Year → Quarter → Month → Day hierarchy, so you can drill from the year into any month.
  • A scatter of hourly against yearly median by role shows whether the two pay scales agree.
  • A matrix by role and quarter with a monthly sparkline fits the trend for every role into one table.
  • Every visual sits on the same 16-pixel grid as a white card on a light grey page, titled with the question it answers.
Salary Star Rating =
VAR __MAX_NUMBER_OF_STARS = 5
VAR __MIN_RATED_VALUE = 75000
VAR __MAX_RATED_VALUE = 150000
VAR __BASE_VALUE = MEDIAN ( 'job_postings_flat'[salary_year_avg] )
-- where the median sits between $75K and $150K, clamped to 0–1
VAR __NORMALIZED_BASE_VALUE =
    MIN ( MAX ( DIVIDE ( __BASE_VALUE - __MIN_RATED_VALUE,
                         __MAX_RATED_VALUE - __MIN_RATED_VALUE ), 0 ), 1 )
VAR __STAR_RATING = ROUND ( __NORMALIZED_BASE_VALUE * __MAX_NUMBER_OF_STARS, 0 )
RETURN
    IF ( NOT ISBLANK ( __BASE_VALUE ),
        REPT ( UNICHAR ( 9733 ), __STAR_RATING )            -- ★
            & REPT ( UNICHAR ( 9734 ), __MAX_NUMBER_OF_STARS - __STAR_RATING ) )  -- ☆

Add a drill-through page

Right-click any role on the dashboard and choose Drill through, or use the button, and a detail page opens filtered to that role. It answers the questions that only make sense one role at a time.

  • Two gauges show where the median yearly and hourly pay sit between that role's minimum and maximum, with the average as the target line.
  • Three donuts: share of postings that are remote, that don't mention a degree, and that offer health insurance.
  • A map sized by number of postings, with median salary in the tooltip.
  • A treemap of schedule types and a bar chart of the job boards the postings came from.

Check the data, then fix what's wrong

Before writing anything up I went back to the raw rows and audited every visual. One issue can only be caveated; the rest are fixed in the report.

  • Salary is rare (caveat). Only 20,335 postings (4.2%) give a yearly salary and 10,076 an hourly one. 84% of the yearly salaries are from the US, which has only 31% of all postings. The pay figures describe the US market more than the global one.
  • “Sudan” wasn't Sudan (fixed). The country column comes from the search location, not the job. Of 10,688 “Sudan” postings, only 36 mention Sudan; the rest are in US cities like Dallas and Austin, or “Anywhere”. A calculated column moves the US ones to the United States and leaves the location-less ones blank, instead of making Sudan the ninth-biggest market on the map.
  • One job board, many names (fixed). “via LinkedIn” and “LinkedIn” were separate values, among 7,817 distinct entries. A second calculated column strips the “via” prefix, which brings it down to 6,541 and shows LinkedIn's real share: 36% of postings.
  • A count that skipped rows (fixed). Both matrices counted postings by country, so any posting without a country went missing from the “Job Count” column. They now count by job title, which every row has.
  • Titles and labels (fixed). The dashboard bar chart was titled “What is the highest paying job in data?” but plots the number of postings, so its title now says so. Two drill-through titles were copied from their neighbours, typos like “Yealy”, “Salarry”, “Titlle” and “haave” are gone, and raw field names like “Count of job_via” now read as plain labels.

06 · Key findings

What the data says

+73% senior DS over analyst

Seniority and ML pay most

Senior Data Scientists ($155.5K) and ML Engineers ($155K) top the medians. Data Analysts are lowest at $90K. Senior Data Analyst ($107K) still sits below the entry-level Data Engineer ($126K).

2 skills in half of all postings

SQL and Python are the baseline

Python (51%) and SQL (50%) are far ahead of cloud skills (AWS 21%, Azure 20%). For analyst roles the order changes: SQL 47%, Excel 33%, Python 32%, then Tableau and Power BI at about 24% each.

27% of postings are engineers

Engineers are the most in demand

Data Engineer is the largest role with 128,994 postings, ahead of Data Analyst (112,866) and Data Scientist (97,664). Add the senior titles and engineering is a third of the market.

+32% remote ML engineers

Remote pays more, except for analysts

Only 13% of postings are remote, and the remote ones pay more overall ($130K vs $113K). Not for analysts, though: remote Data Analyst jobs pay $85K against $90K on site, and remote Senior Data Analyst jobs pay less too.

38.5% of analyst postings

Analyst roles are the most open

Over a third of Data Analyst and Data Engineer postings don't mention a degree, and those postings pay only slightly less ($110K vs $114K median). For Data Scientists it's 7%, and for ML Engineers 4%.

−59% Q4 vs Q1

Postings fell through the year

156,645 postings in Q1 against 63,591 in Q4, with a low of 13,719 in November. Some of that is hiring seasonality, but a fall this sharp may also mean the data was collected differently late in the year.

07 · Recommendations

What I'd do with this

  1. Learn SQL and Python first. They're in half of all postings, whatever the role. Everything else is second.
  2. For analyst roles, pair them with a BI tool. Excel, Tableau and Power BI each appear in a quarter to a third of analyst postings.
  3. Treat engineering as the growth path. It's the largest role and pays $36K more at the median than analysis.
  4. Don't rule out roles over a degree. A third of analyst and engineering postings don't ask for one, at almost the same pay.
  5. Read the salary charts as US figures. More than four in five salaried postings are American, so check local data before using them elsewhere.
  6. Add a second year. A 2025 extract would show whether the drop in Q4 was the job market or a change in how the postings were collected.

08 · Reflection

What I learned

Know the chart before you need it

Building a page for each visual type first meant I picked charts for the dashboard on purpose: a scatter to compare two pay scales, gauges for one value within a range, a matrix with sparklines for many trends at once.

Count what's missing

A median of $113K looks like a fact until you see that 96% of postings have no salary. Now I check how many rows a number is actually based on before I chart it.

Drill-through keeps pages simple

Moving the per-role detail to its own page let the dashboard stay uncluttered. Each page answers one level of question, and the user chooses when to go deeper.
